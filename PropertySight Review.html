Product & Conversion Review · Prepared as a first-time prospect

PropertySight, judged by someone deciding whether to trust it, pay for it, and run their business on it.

A skeptical walk through the marketing site and the mobile app — from the five-second first impression to the subscription decision and beyond. Every criticism is tied to something actually in the site copy or the app's own code and strings.

Reviewer lens: licensed U.S. agent, never heard of the product Surfaces: marketing site + Flutter app Date: July 2026
5.8/10
Overall

The product underneath is genuinely differentiated — a point-your-camera-at-a-house lead scanner with honest, well-sourced data and refund-on-no-data integrity. But the funnel is leaking at almost every join: the site never shows a price, every download button is a dead link, there is zero social proof, and the app makes a new agent subscribe before it will reveal an owner or a value. Great engine, unfinished go-to-market.

Scores are directional judgments from one prospect's walkthrough, not statistically sampled. They exist to prioritize, not to grade.

Scorecard at a glance

First impression & value proposition

In five seconds I can tell this is a real-estate scouting tool, and the "point your phone at a house" hook is strong and unusual. What I can't tell is whether it works, what it costs, or whether anyone else uses it.

The hero does the most important job well: "point your phone at any house to pull the owner, the assessor value, and lead signals." That is a concrete, novel promise — most competitors make you type an address into a desktop database. The three-part "Look it up / Log it by address / Pull it up later" framing is clear and benefit-led.

Where it falls short of a confident buy:

  • No proof it's real. There is no screenshot gallery beyond the stylized phone mockups, no demo video, and — critically — no numbers. How many agents use it? How many properties are covered? A skeptic assumes "brand new, unproven."
  • The "why me" is implied, not stated. The site never names the alternative it beats (a clipboard, PropStream/BatchLeads on a laptop, driving-for-dollars apps). A prospect has to construct the competitive argument themselves.
  • No measurable benefit. Nothing quantifies the payoff — "find X more off-market deals," "cut list-building from an evening to a drive." The value is described, never sized.
😐 5s: "Oh, a scanner for houses — interesting" 🤔 30s: "…but is this a real company?" 😟 60s: "Why won't it tell me the price?"

Website review

Visually the site is clean, modern, and on-brand — a calm navy/sky-blue palette, good type rhythm, real scroll-story structure (Finds → Signals → Pricing → Activity). Craft is not the problem. Completeness and trust are. The most damaging issues:

Critical · The download buttons don't work
Every App Store / Google Play badge and every "Download for iOS/Android" footer link points at href="#". A prospect who is finally convinced literally cannot get the app. This is a 100% leak at the single most important click on the page.
Critical · There is no price anywhere on the site
The "Pricing" section explains the credit/unlock tiers but never states a dollar figure. The actual plan — $9.99/mo Starter (35 scans + 3 credits), a Pro tier (75 scans + 5 credits), top-up packs from $9.99 — exists only inside the app. Forcing a prospect to install, create an account, and hit a paywall just to learn the price is a major, avoidable drop-off, and it reads as evasive.
High · Zero social proof or trust signals
No testimonials, no agent logos or brokerages, no ratings, no "as seen in," no founder/about, no case study, no FAQ. For a tool an agent would wire into their livelihood, the absence of any human validation is the difference between "interesting" and "trustworthy."

Other website gaps that cost trust or conversion:

  • No Terms of Service and no refund policy page. Only Privacy is linked. Ironically, refund-on-unverified-data is one of the product's best trust stories (it's real, and enforced in code) — yet it's buried in one sentence instead of being a headline.
  • Support is a lone mailto:. No help center, no response-time promise, no phone, no chat. A business buyer wants to know someone answers.
  • No FAQ. The obvious objections — "Is this legal? Where's the data from? What's a credit vs a scan? What happens to my data if I stop paying?" — go unanswered, so the prospect answers them pessimistically.
  • The Sources page is a genuine asset and should be promoted, not tucked in the nav. Naming BatchData + Google and explaining DNC flagging is exactly the credibility a skeptic wants.
What's working on the site
Strong central hook; clean, professional visual design; honest data-sourcing page; the credit-tier model is explained without hype; and the "refunded if unverified" promise is a real differentiator. The bones are good — it's under-finished, not badly built.

App & onboarding review

The app is where PropertySight earns its keep, and the core loop — scan a house, get records, save it, work it later — is coherent and well-instrumented. The friction is concentrated in the money model and in one retention decision that will scare people.

Onboarding & first-time experience

  • Sign-in is Google/Apple only. Fast and low-friction, and guest mode was removed for a defensible anti-abuse reason — but there's no email/password option, which quietly excludes agents who don't want to link a personal Google/Apple identity to a work tool.
  • Only 2 free scans before the wall. The trial grants two scans, and trial users see owner/value/financials locked behind "Subscribe to unlock." So a prospect's "first success" is deliberately incomplete — they scan a house and are told to pay to see what they actually came for. Time-to-real-value is effectively "after purchase," which is a hard sell on a tool you haven't proven yet.
  • The tutorial is solid. A four-step first-launch tour plus a fuller "How it works" (8 sections) and a Signal Guide exist. Discoverability of features is above average.

The two-currency model is the biggest usability tax

There are two separate currencies: "scans" and "credits." Scanning a property costs a scan; unlocking deeper data costs credits (Financials 1, Permits+Distress 1, Contact 1, Comps 2, or a 3-credit bundle). Subscriptions grant a monthly allotment of both, top-ups add credits, and there's even a "watch ads to earn" path and a "convert 1 credit to 5 scans" dialog. A new user cannot hold this model in their head. Every dual-currency system taxes comprehension; here it sits directly on top of the purchase decision.

Critical · Your saved finds are deleted if you stop subscribing
The app's own strings warn ex-subscribers and trial users that their finds — photos, notes, unlocked owner data — will be deleted on a sunset timer unless they subscribe. That means the CRM an agent builds is hostage to the subscription. It's a legitimate business lever, but to a prospect evaluating trust it reads as "if I ever pause, I lose my pipeline." This will surface in reviews and word-of-mouth and suppress both adoption and referrals.
Medium · "Watch ads to earn credits" undercuts the premium framing
An ad-for-credits path on a professional lead tool cheapens it. Agents comparing this to a $99–$199/mo data platform may read the ad prompt as "consumer app," not "business tool," and trust the data less by association.

Smaller app observations

  • Refund-on-no-data is everywhere and it's excellent — every unlock refunds the credit if the record is empty. This is the app's strongest trust feature.
  • Storage caps + grace expiry mean a heavy user can hit a wall and be pushed to "clean up or upgrade" — fine, but another place a saved find can vanish.
  • Owner Contact requires an active subscription even if you have credits — correctly disclosed in-app now, but a surprise the first time.
  • Errors are swallowed to a generic "Couldn't sign in." Safe, but gives a stuck user nothing to act on.
  • Brand naming drift: internal defaults still say "Seepopi / Point. Identify. Explore." while the site is "PropertySight / Point · Scan · Discover." Make sure production never shows the old name.
  • Property data gaps (new construction, re-parceled lots) are handled honestly with a clear "BatchData doesn't have this yet" message. Good.

Pricing, trust & competitive position

Would I subscribe with my own money?

At $9.99/mo, the entry price is almost an impulse buy and objectively cheap next to PropStream/BatchLeads-class tools. That's a strength the site throws away by hiding it. The objections that would still stop me: (1) I can't judge value because I never saw the data quality before paying; (2) the scan/credit math makes the true cost-per-lead opaque; (3) the "lose your finds if you cancel" policy makes the subscription feel like a trap rather than a tool. Missing entirely: an annual option's real savings, a clear "what a typical month costs," and any money-back framing beyond the per-unlock refund.

Competitive position

  • Advantages: the camera-first, in-the-field capture is a real wedge; the price is disruptive; the sourcing transparency and DNC/refund integrity are better than much of the category.
  • Where competitors look better: they show pricing, reviews, and demos openly; they let you keep exported data; they offer skip-tracing without a scavenger-hunt of currencies; and they have brand trust PropertySight hasn't built yet.
Trust verdict
Would I run my business on it today? Not yet — but close. The data integrity is there; the company signals aren't. Fix the missing price, add proof, publish terms/refund/support, and soften the data-deletion story, and this moves from "skeptical" to "worth a try" quickly.

How the experience feels, stage by stage

  • Landing (interested): the scan hook is genuinely cool — I lean in.
  • Scrolling (skeptical): polished, but where's the price, the proof, the people? Doubt creeps in.
  • Trying to download (frustrated): the button does nothing. Momentum dies.
  • Onboarding (cautiously optimistic): quick sign-in, nice tutorial — hopeful again.
  • First scan (deflated): "Subscribe to see owner & value." I came for exactly that.
  • Paywall (wary): cheap price, but a currency puzzle and a "lose your data if you leave" undertone.

Executive summary

PropertySight is a good product wearing an unfinished go-to-market. The core idea — turn a phone camera into a curbside property-and-owner scanner, then let an agent build a private, address-anchored follow-up list — is differentiated, timely, and genuinely useful for the driving-for-dollars and off-market-listing workflows agents already run. The engineering shows real integrity: data sources are named honestly, skip-traced contacts are flagged against the National Do Not Call registry, and unverified lookups are automatically refunded. Those are trust assets most competitors don't bother with.

Would I use it? Yes, cautiously — the core loop is worth a try. Would I recommend it? Not yet, because I couldn't confidently vouch for a company that hides its price, can't be downloaded from its own site, and threatens to delete the data I create. Would I pay for it? At $9.99/mo, very possibly — if I could first see one full result and trust that my list stays mine.

The three biggest reasons someone won't become a customer: (1) they literally can't — the download links are dead and the price is invisible until deep in the app; (2) they can't prove value before paying, because the trial locks the very data the product promises; (3) they don't trust a young brand with no reviews, no terms, thin support, and a policy that deletes their work if they stop paying.

The three biggest strengths: (1) a differentiated, camera-first capture experience with no obvious equal at this price; (2) honest, well-sourced data with DNC compliance and refund-on-no-data baked in; (3) clean, professional design across both surfaces that signals competence.

If I had two weeks: fix the four cheapest, highest-impact leaks — wire the download buttons, publish pricing on the site, let the first scan or two reveal a complete result, and change the retention model so canceling downgrades access rather than deleting the agent's finds. None require a rebuild; together they repair the two worst joints in the funnel (site→install and first-scan→value) and remove the loudest trust objection.

Five highest-ROI improvements: real download links; on-site pricing with a worked example; a full-value first scan; keep-your-data-after-cancel; and social proof plus a short demo video. In that order.

What investors would raise: Is retention propped up by hostage-taking (delete-on-cancel) rather than by love of the product — and what's true voluntary retention? Is the $9.99 price and ad-for-credits path a margin problem given per-lookup data costs from BatchData? How defensible is this if a well-funded incumbent bolts a camera onto an existing data platform? And why is measured conversion low despite a strong hook — is it demand, or the self-inflicted funnel leaks documented here?

Estimated conversion, and the opportunity. With dead download links, no visible price, and a paywalled trial, a realistic visitor→subscriber rate today is roughly 0.5–1.5% — and much of that loss is mechanical, not demand-driven. Reasoning: a strong hook likely earns a healthy visitor→intent rate, but the dead CTA and hidden price throw away a large share before install; then the locked trial sheds another big chunk before anyone experiences value. Because these are self-inflicted and cheap to fix, the upside is unusually large: simply making the buttons work, showing the price, and letting the first scan deliver a full result could plausibly lift end-to-end conversion several-fold before touching demand generation. The rare, encouraging situation here is that the product is ahead of its funnel — the fastest wins are in plumbing, not in the pitch.
